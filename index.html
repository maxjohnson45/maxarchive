Introduction

The Cathedral of Learning, the most notable building on the University of Pittsburgh’s campus, contains 31 different nationality rooms, celebrating cultures all around the world with beautiful architecture. An article from the 1992 Scandinavian Journal of History claims the architects faced the challenge of wondering “How can you present your cultural background in the form of a university classroom?” (Bjorgum) The archive that I have selected dates back to March 17, 1939. At this point in time, only five of the now thirty one nationality rooms had been completed, and this document goes in detail about the rooms that architects had finished at the time, as well as the rooms in progress and which rooms they plan to make for the future. .

What is this document?

This document details the process of how the nationality rooms came to be and the progress that has been made since the project started in 1926. A 2020 article by Mary Springer discusses how “Before the Great Depression, Pittsburgh prioritized industry over culture,” (Springer). This makes it interesting to see how the architects put much care into building the Cathedral itself, let alone filling the rooms with culture from all around the world. The document provides photos and descriptions of the Swedish, German, Scottish, Russian, and Early American rooms. It also lists 13 rooms in progress at the time, including the Italian room, which officially debuted in 1949. Since 1987, twelve rooms have been added to the Cathedral, some of which have gone up to the third floor. (Pitt). The document also makes a point to show how the windows of the rooms show off and highlight the culture of Pittsburgh as well. The preservation of the rooms in their original states emphasizes why this document is important in the first place. Understanding where the rooms came from gives a greater appreciation of the rooms themselves and also the timeless culture behind them. .

What is the meaning of the document?

The document details the creation of the nationality rooms in order to inform students and Pittsburgh locals of the newly thought of project and explain how the idea came about, what the rooms mean, and future plans for potential additions. Now, it serves an additional purpose many years later. It still provides the initial history of the rooms, but anyone who reads this document has that knowledge alongside the knowledge of what the rooms have become in the present day. The document has found a new use several years later, as it serves as a time capsule from the beginning of the project, allowing for an easy comparison to be made between the present day and almost a century ago. .

Why is this document important?

This archive documents an important part in the University’s history and highlights how important culture remains to the University and to the world as a whole even eighty five years ago. Many students at Pitt either walk past or have a class in one of these rooms everyday, and seeing this document and understanding the creative process allows for appreciation of this culture to persist into the modern day. The document would especially be interesting to students taking classes in those rooms, as the nationality rooms differ from the stereotypical classrooms that you may find anywhere else on campus. The uniqueness of the rooms may make a student interested in researching further about them, and that research may lead them to this very archive..

What questions does this document raise?

This archive raises the question of how these rooms would look if the architects thought of the idea in the present day rather than almost a hundred years ago. If one compares the picture of the German room from the document to a present day picture of the German room from Pitt’s website, you would see that the layout and architecture of the room have not changed one bit. If that room along with the other four original rooms were made today, they may have been designed differently, as cultures always grow and change. The question provokes thought because while it may be designed differently today architecture-wise, the preservation of the culture itself would remain. The representation created by the different rooms and the intended message behind them would remain prevalent regardless of what era of time the rooms encapsulate. The document also makes students at Pitt think about all the history that existed on campus before they enrolled, and how these rooms opened long before they arrived and will stay the same long after they graduate. The document allows for students such as myself to appreciate the history and culture of the college they go to, and maybe have more pride about where they came from themselves. The document also raises the question of whether or not the design of the room actually positively impacts the student compared to any other standard room on Pitt’s campus. Research from Educause states that “the physical characteristics of learning environments can affect learners emotionally, with important cognitive and behavioral consequences.” Students will likely have a harder time getting work done or learning things in the comfort of their own room, but they would also have trouble in a bland room that does not fit their style of learning. Knowing this information, makes one question whether or not there are any psychological benefits to the rooms and their designs. .
